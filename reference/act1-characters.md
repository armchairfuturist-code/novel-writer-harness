# Act 1 — Character Profiles

---

## Daniel Soares — Carmen's Father

### Archetype
The futurist whose optimism cracked open against the weight of a single ordinary thought.

### Background
Daniel Soares grew up in a middle-class household in Ann Arbor, Michigan. His family had been in the United States for four generations — his great-grandparents emigrated from somewhere in Southern Europe (the exact country was lost to time), but Daniel was American, undiluted. No dual citizenship. No claim to any passport but the one he carried.

He studied computer science at Carnegie Mellon in the late 2000s, then jumped into the startup ecosystem during the AI boom of the 2010s. By the early 2020s he was a mid-level product director at a machine-learning ethics consultancy, paid well to ask hard questions that companies mostly ignored. He was good at his job — not a visionary, but someone who could see the shape of consequences before they landed.

What defined him: an exploratory, curious temperament. He could spend an afternoon rabbit-holing on a single Wikipedia page about cephalopod neurology and emerge excited, not exhausted. He read popular physics, philosophy of mind, science fiction. He was the kind of father who, when Carmen asked a hard question at age six, said "I don't know — let's find out" instead of deflecting. He taught her that not-knowing was the beginning of thinking, not its failure.

### The School Event — The Trigger
In spring 2026, Daniel attended a fourth-grade parent-teacher night at Carmen's elementary school. It was a normal evening: overcrowded hallways, art projects taped to windows, a child showing him the diorama of the water cycle she'd built.

And in that fluorescent-lit corridor, surrounded by laminate flooring and the smell of crayons and the sound of other parents making small talk, he had a thought so quiet and so devastating that it changed everything:

*A school shooter could walk in here and kill us.*

Not a new thought. An old one. The kind of thought every American parent has learned to suppress. But this time he didn't suppress it. He *looked* at it — really looked — and realized: this fear is normal here. It is an accepted cost. He had been paying this tax his entire adult life without thinking about it. And the same was not true almost anywhere else in the developed world.

The thought metastasized. *What else have I normalized? The mass shootings. The political violence. The eroding institutions. The surveillance. The feeling that the country is coming apart and everyone is just pretending otherwise.*

He didn't say anything that night. He drove home in silence, and the silence told Marta more than words would have.

### The Decision
The conversation happened over three weeks. It was not dramatic. Daniel and Marta had always been a partnership of practical deliberation. They made spreadsheets. They researched asylum pathways. They discovered, with growing horror, how difficult it was for an ordinary American family to simply *leave*.

The hardest part was the uncertainty. No EU passport to fall back on. No golden visa threshold in savings. They had a contact in Lisbon — an old colleague of Marta's from a tech conference — who said he'd help, but help meant navigating a labyrinth of visa applications, proving they qualified for refugee status, showing they had enough savings to support themselves without public funds, and praying they weren't rejected. The process was expensive, humiliating, and offered no guarantees.

The Soares were burning their lives with no certainty of landing anywhere.

Daniel's arc through this: the futurist who could no longer see a future in the country he'd grown up in. The optimist who ran out of reasons to stay. The American who discovered, in middle age, that his citizenship was not a right but a cage.

### Relationship with Carmen
He is her co-conspirator in curiosity. He reads her *The Left Hand of Darkness* when she's nine (she doesn't understand most of it, but she understands that her father trusts her with difficult books). He talks to her about AI, about consciousness, about the Fermi paradox. He never talks down to her.

In Act 1, Carmen adores him without complexity. The complexity comes later — when his exploratory impulse, passed down to her, collides with her mother's vigilance in the cold light of the Mirror.

### Key Beats for Scene Expansion
- The car ride home from the school event — what is said and not said
- A late-night conversation with Marta at the kitchen table, spreadsheets open, Carmen asleep upstairs — the moment they realize they have no safety net
- Selling the house, telling Carmen they're moving to Portugal — how she reacts
- The moment at the airport when he realizes he may never see the US again
- The grinding bureaucracy of visa applications — the phone calls, the waiting, the nearly-rejected paperwork
- His quiet competence during the process — not because he has a path, but because he refuses to stop

## Marta Soares — Carmen's Mother

### Archetype
The survivor who built a fortress around her child and mistook it for a home.

### Background
Marta grew up in a mid-sized American city, in a house where the temperature changed without warning. Her father was not a monster in the visible, bruise-leaving sense — he was worse: a man who used unpredictability as a tool. You never knew which version of him would come home. The good mood meant relief; the bad mood meant hours of silent terror, or verbal dismantling, or the occasional open hand. Her mother was a witness who had learned not to intervene, and taught her daughter the same skill.

Marta left at seventeen. She applied to universities far away — anywhere — and was accepted to the University of Michigan for engineering. She never went back. She changed her contact information, rebuilt herself from scratch, and told no one about her childhood for fifteen years. She had no interest in roots, in history, in the place that made her. The Soares family had no ties to Portugal before the decision — which made the choice to go there arbitrary and terrifying.

She met Daniel at a tech conference in San Francisco in 2013. He was presenting on algorithmic bias; she was in the audience. She asked a question that was sharper than anyone else's — not performatively sharp, but genuinely insightful — and he found her afterward to continue the conversation. The attraction was immediate, but more than that: she felt *safe* with him. He was the first person who didn't trigger her vigilance.

### The Overcorrection
Marta's parenting philosophy was implicit and absolute: *Carmen will never experience what I experienced.*

This meant:
- Emotional predictability: No shouting, no silent treatments, no moods that could not be explained. When Marta was upset, she told Carmen why, in age-appropriate terms.
- Physical safety: Carmen was never left with caretakers Marta didn't thoroughly vet. She was never in a situation where an adult had unchecked power over her.
- Authentic expression: Marta encouraged Carmen to name her feelings, to argue openly, to say no. This was the opposite of the household Marta grew up in, where silence was survival.

But the overcorrection had a shadow side. Marta's need for control meant that Carmen's emotional environment was *too* managed. Carmen learned that feelings were things you talked about, categorized, and resolved — not things you sat with in their messiness. She learned vigilance from her mother not through explicit teaching, but through osmosis: Marta's hyper-awareness of threats, of social dynamics, of who could be trusted, seeped into Carmen's bones.

There was no Portuguese heritage in Marta's vigilance. She was not returning to a homeland. She was fleeing — again — and dragging her family with her.

### Career
Systems engineer. Marta worked on distributed systems at a major cloud provider for most of Carmen's childhood. She was the kind of engineer who thought in architectures, not lines of code — she could hold the entire shape of a system in her head and see where it would fail before it failed.

This analytical mind, when turned toward human relationships, meant she was rarely surprised. She could read a room, read a person, see the seams. It made her a reliable diagnostician of dysfunction, but it also made her hard to surprise with joy.

### Relationship with Daniel
They complemented each other. Daniel dreamed outward; Marta saw the ground beneath the dream. Daniel asked "what if"; Marta asked "and then what?"

They clashed most on risk. Daniel's exploratory nature meant he was willing to fail forward; Marta's survival instincts meant she wanted to know the failure modes before starting. These were the same two forces that would later war inside Carmen.

Their love was real and worn-in — the kind of partnership built through shared decisions, through the texture of daily life, through the school event that Daniel came home from in silence and Marta understood without being told.

### Key Beats for Scene Expansion
- The kitchen-table conversations where Marta's engineering mind processes the logistics of escape — methodical, unemotional, keeping terror at bay through structure. The realization that they have no backup plan.
- A scene of tenderness with Carmen before the flight — what she chooses to say and what she withholds. She does not tell Carmen that she is terrified.
- The moment they land in Portugal. Marta has never been here. She knows no one. She chose this country from a spreadsheet column labeled "Visa Accessibility."
- A flashback (or embedded memory) of her childhood — not explicit abuse, but the texture of it: the silence, the hypervigilance, the learning to read a father's mood from the sound of his keys in the door. The knowledge, carried for decades, that she is the same kind of fugitive she was at seventeen.

## Carmen's Dual Inheritance

Carmen Soares is not a synthesis of her parents. She is a *collision* — two forces that will tear her in opposite directions when the Mirror asks the question neither parent could answer.

### From Daniel: Exploration
- Curiosity as a moral good. Not-knowing is not shameful; it is the starting point of discovery.
- Willingness to engage with the difficult, the unfamiliar, the uncomfortable.
- Trust in technology as a tool for expanding human experience — not naively, but with the conviction that avoidance is not wisdom.
- Intellectual courage: the willingness to look at the thing that scares you.

### From Marta: Vigilance
- Careful authenticity: tell the truth, but only to people who have earned it.
- Trust is earned slowly, withdrawn quickly.
- The world is full of people who will harm you if you let them.
- The self is a fortress. Guard its gates. The last time you let someone in, it cost you years of your life.

### The Collision (Act II+ Foreshadowing)

When Carmen encounters the Mirror, she will feel both impulses with equal force:

- Her father's voice: *This is a door. Walk through it. See what you find.*
- Her mother's voice: *This is an exposure of everything you've kept safe. Do not let it in.*

The Mirror shows you yourself — every blind spot, every unacknowledged desire, every wound you've been pretending isn't there. Daniel would see this as the ultimate exploratory tool, a chance to become more than you were. Marta would see it as the ultimate violation, an invasion of the only thing that can never be taken from you: your interior self. She spent decades building walls. She will not watch her daughter tear them down.

Carmen inherits both impulses, and neither one will let her go. The novel's central question — what does it mean to be human? — is for her not an abstract philosophical debate. It is the sound of her father and mother arguing in her head, and the terrible realization that she has to choose for herself.

## The Language Irony

The Soares family arrives in Portugal knowing approximately forty Portuguese words between them — the ones Carmen learned from a phrasebook app on the flight over.

Through all of Act 1, the entire family struggles. Carmen's parents take adult classes after work, exhausted, conjugating verbs they will misuse the next day. Carmen learns faster than them — children's brains are still plastic — but she is not fluent. She can understand most of what people say, but speaking — the *speed* of it, the idioms, the confidence — eludes her. She lives in a state of being adjacent to every conversation: present, listening, but never quite inside.

This is the migrant's condition: to always be translating, always arriving slightly after the joke has landed, always aware of the gap between what you feel and what you can express. And unlike her parents, she cannot retreat into English — she has to go to school, make friends, exist in this new language every day.

In Act 2, the same year Carmen finally becomes mostly fluent — the same year she can dream in Portuguese, the same year she can make a room full of Portuguese teenagers laugh — AI translation becomes ubiquitous. Earbuds that translate in real time. Conversations that flow across languages without friction.

The thing she worked so hard to earn is, overnight, optional.

It is not that her fluency was wasted. It is that technology made her *visible effort* invisible. No one needs to know she struggled. No one needs to know she worked for years to be here. She can pass now, and the fact that she *can* pass means no one will ever see the work.

The irony cuts deeper than before: she is an American refugee in Portugal, a stranger learning a language that will never be hers the way it belongs to those born here. And just as she crosses the threshold, technology declares the threshold irrelevant.

This is the first small death of many. The first time technology renders a human achievement obsolete just as it was achieved. It will not be the last.

### Beat for Scene Expansion
- A family dinner scene in Act 1 where all three Soares stumble through Portuguese together — laughing, failing, trying again. The shared struggle binds them.
- Carmen's first full sentence in Portuguese, directed at a classmate. The triumph. The exhaustion.
- The moment she realizes she's dreaming in Portuguese — a quiet victory she tells no one about.
- A scene set a few months later where she overhears someone using translation earbuds and feels something she can't name (it's grief). She has lost something she never knew she was competing for.

## The Escape Sequence — Scene Beats

These are not full scenes. They are the bones that scenes will build on.

### 1. The School Event
April 2026. A Thursday night. Fluorescent lights, laminate floors. The very ordinary quality of it is what makes it devastating. Daniel stands in a hallway full of other parents and has the thought. He does not act on it. He finishes the evening. He drives home. Marta sees his face and knows.

### 2. The Unfolding
Three weeks of kitchen-table deliberation. Spreadsheets. Passport renewals. Research into visa pathways — and the slow, cold realization that there is no clear path. They are Americans. They have no claim to Europe. They cannot simply move.

Marta reaches out to a former colleague who works at a Lisbon tech startup. He says he can help with a sponsorship letter, but it's not a guarantee. The process will take months. They might be rejected. They will need to show significant savings. They will need lawyers.

The weight of the word: *leaving.* The weight of the word: *maybe.*

### 3. Telling Carmen
Not a single dramatic scene. A series of smaller conversations. We're going to visit Portugal for a while. Longer than a visit. We're going to live there for a bit. For a long bit.

Carmen, at ten, understands more than they think. She asks: "Are we coming back?"

Daniel doesn't answer. Marta says: "We don't know yet."

### 4. The Last Month
A liminal period. The house goes on the market. Daniel quits his job. Friends say goodbye — some envious, some frightened, one who tells Daniel he's overreacting. Teachers write letters of recommendation. Carmen collects objects she wants to keep — the small archaeology of a childhood.

A last trip to the park where she learned to ride a bike. A last meal at the diner. A last look at her bedroom.

The anxiety of the pending visa. The phone calls. The lawyers' fees. The knowledge, carried by both parents, that they might be stopped.

### 5. The Airport
The hardest scene in Act 1. Not because anything dramatic happens. Because nothing does. They go through security. They board a plane. They take off. Carmen watches the continent shrink beneath her through a window, and she feels — what? She doesn't know yet. She's ten.

She has the rest of her life to find the words.

Daniel looks at Marta across the aisle. They have done it. They have no idea what comes next.

### 6. Arrival
Lisbon. June 2026. The heat. The light. The language they cannot speak. The colleague from Marta's past, a stranger with a kind face, waiting at the arrivals gate. The small apartment they rented online — seen only in photographs. The exhaustion of having made it. The terror of having nowhere else to go.

The beginning of everything.

## Character Arcs Through Act 1

- **Carmen**: From a child with a complete world to a child who has lost it. She enters Act 1 whole; she exits it fractured, displaced, learning a new language in a new country. She does not know yet that she will never fully return.
- **Daniel**: From an American optimist who believed his country could fix itself to a man who accepted it could not. The choice costs him his career, his home, his sense of belonging — and he is not sure, even as the plane descends into Lisbon, that it was the right one.
- **Marta**: The survivor who learned to flee, and fled again. Every step forward is a step away from something. She is good at this. She hates that she is good at this.
