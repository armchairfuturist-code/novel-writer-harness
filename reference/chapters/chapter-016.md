# Chapter 16: The Fracture

**POV: Marta Soares | August 2032 | Lisbon, Portugal**

---

Marta woke from a dream she could not remember, her heart hammering, the sheets twisted around her legs. The room was dark. Daniel was asleep beside her, breathing evenly. The clock on the nightstand said 3:47 AM.

She lay still and tried to slow her breathing. The dream was gone, but the feeling of it remained — a residue of fear that clung to her like sweat. She knew this feeling. She had known it her whole life. It was the feeling of being hunted by something she could not name.

She got up. She moved through the dark house with the silence of someone who had learned, very young, that making noise could be dangerous. She made tea. She sat at the kitchen table in the dark and watched the steam rise from the cup.

The tea was chamomile. It did not help.

---

The incident happened three days later.

Marta was at the supermarket. A routine trip — milk, bread, the things that ran out on schedule. She was standing in the produce section, choosing tomatoes, when she noticed a woman at the end of the aisle. The woman was wearing Mirror earbuds — the new model, smaller than the original, almost invisible. She was talking to someone, or to herself, her lips moving in a conversation that Marta could not hear.

She was also watching Marta.

Not watching, exactly. Looking in her direction. Her eyes were unfocused, the way eyes were when the Mirror was feeding you information. But her head was turned toward Marta, and she was smiling.

Marta's chest tightened. She put the tomatoes down.

She walked to the next aisle. The woman followed.

She turned into the dairy section. The woman appeared at the end of the aisle, still smiling, still watching.

Marta's breath came faster. She abandoned her cart. She walked to the checkout, her hands shaking, and stood in line with nothing to buy. The woman did not follow her to the checkout. But Marta could feel her — could feel the attention, the observation, the invisible thread of someone else's awareness wrapped around her.

She paid for nothing. She walked out of the store. She stood in the parking lot in the August heat, her hands pressed against her knees, trying not to hyperventilate.

The woman was not there. The parking lot was empty.

Marta stood in the sun and told herself she was being irrational. The woman had not been following her. The Mirror earbuds had not been aimed at her. She was projecting. She was paranoid. She was letting her childhood leak into the present, the way it always did, the way she had never learned to stop.

She told herself all of this.

She did not believe any of it.

---

She did not tell Daniel. She did not tell Carmen. She sat through dinner with a smile that felt like a mask, and when Daniel asked if she was okay, she said she was tired.

The lie tasted familiar. She had been telling it for decades.

---

That night, she could not sleep. She lay beside Daniel and listened to his breathing and thought about the woman in the supermarket. The way her eyes had tracked Marta. The smile that had not been friendly.

She knew, rationally, that it had been nothing. A coincidence. A stranger looking in her direction. She had seen a thousand strangers in a thousand supermarkets, and none of them had been a threat.

But the feeling would not leave. The feeling was older than reason. It had been with her since she was seven years old, since she had learned that the world was not safe, that the people who were supposed to protect you could be the ones you needed protection from.

She closed her eyes. The darkness behind her eyelids was not empty. It was full of things she had spent her life trying not to see.

---

She was born in São Paulo, in a neighborhood where the police did not go. Her father was a man who believed that love and violence were the same thing, expressed differently depending on the situation. Her mother was a woman who had learned to be invisible.

Marta learned both lessons.

She learned that love was conditional and could be withdrawn at any moment. She learned that safety was an illusion, that the walls of your home could become the walls of your prison. She learned to read the mood of a room the way a sailor reads the weather — by watching for signs, by feeling the pressure change, by knowing when to make herself small.

She left at seventeen. She never went back. She changed her name. She changed her accent. She built a life from scratch, in a country where no one knew her, and she told herself that the past was the past and that she had escaped it.

She had not escaped it. She had carried it inside her, the way you carry a splinter that has healed over. The flesh seals around it. The pain becomes background. But it is still there. It is always still there.

---

Daniel noticed. Of course he noticed. He had been married to her for twenty years.

"Marta. Talk to me."

They were in the bedroom. Daniel was sitting on the edge of the bed, his hands on his knees, his face open in the way that had made her fall in love with him a lifetime ago.

"There's nothing to talk about."

"Something happened."

"Nothing happened."

"Marta."

She felt the walls rising inside her. The familiar architecture of deflection. *If I don't say it, it isn't real. If I don't name it, it has no power.* She had built her life on that principle.

But the walls were not working. They had not been working for months. The Mirror was everywhere now — on the news, in conversations, in the supermarket. It was in her daughter's eyes. It was in her husband's quiet enthusiasm. It was in the air she breathed, and she could not escape it.

"There was a woman," she said. "At the supermarket. She was wearing Mirror earbuds. She was watching me."

Daniel waited.

"I know it sounds crazy. I know she probably wasn't watching me. I know I'm projecting. But I can't —" Her voice cracked. "I can't stop feeling like something is coming for me. Like the thing I've been running from my whole life has finally caught up."

"Can I tell you something?"

She looked at him.

"I've been using the Mirror for over a year. And one of the things it showed me was that I married someone who was running as fast as I was, just in the opposite direction."

She stared at him.

"I ran toward the future. You ran away from the past. And we met somewhere in the middle, and we built a life, and I thought that was enough." His voice was gentle. "But you've never stopped running, Marta. And I don't think you know how."

---

The confrontation came two days later.

They were in the kitchen. Carmen was at school. Daniel had come home early from work.

"I want you to try the Mirror," he said.

"No."

"Just once. One session. I'll be with you the whole time."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I know what I'll find."

"What will you find?"

She did not answer. She could not answer. The truth was too large to fit into words. What she would find was herself — the version of herself she had been running from for forty years. The child who had learned that love hurt. The teenager who had escaped but never healed. The woman who had built walls so high and so thick that she had forgotten there was a world outside them.

She would find that she had never stopped being afraid.

"You don't understand," she said. "The Mirror is not a tool to me. It's a threat. It's a machine that will take the thing I've spent my whole life protecting — my inner life — and hand it back to me in a shape I don't recognize."

"That sounds like healing."

"That sounds like destruction."

He looked at her. She could see the love in his eyes, and the sadness, and the exhaustion of twenty years of loving someone who would not let herself be loved.

"Marta," he said. "I don't know how much longer we can live like this."

"Like what?"

"Like you're still running. Like the past is still happening. Like the walls you built are the only thing keeping you safe."

She wanted to tell him that the walls were not a choice. That they had been built by a child who had no other option. That she had spent her entire adult life trying to find a door in them and had never found one.

But the words would not come. They were stuck behind the walls, the same walls, the ones she had built when she was seven years old and had never learned to take down.

---

She found Carmen's Mirror headband that evening.

It was in Carmen's room, on her desk, hidden under a notebook. Marta had not been looking for it. She had been looking for something else — a book, a pen, a reason to be in her daughter's room. But there it was. The sleek curve of it. The sensor pad. The thing that had been changing her daughter without Marta's permission.

She picked it up. It was lighter than she expected.

She thought about putting it on. She thought about seeing what Carmen had seen. About understanding what had drawn her daughter into this world. About facing the thing she had been running from.

She held the headband in her hands for a long time.

Then she put it back under the notebook and left the room.

She was not ready. She did not know if she would ever be ready.

---

That night, she lay awake and listened to Daniel breathe. She thought about the woman in the supermarket. She thought about the headband under the notebook. She thought about the walls she had built and the life she had made inside them.

She realized, with the clarity that comes at four in the morning, that she had been running her whole life. From her father. From the neighborhood. From the girl she had been. From the possibility of being seen. From the terror of being known.

The Mirror was not a threat to her walls. It was a door. And she had spent so long building walls that she had forgotten what doors were for.

She did not know if she could walk through it.

But for the first time in forty years, she understood that the door existed.

She turned onto her side. The tears came silently, the way they had learned to come in childhood — without sound, without movement, without any sign that they were happening at all.

She let them come.

She did not know if she could stop running. But she knew, for the first time, that she wanted to.

And wanting to stop was not the same as stopping. But it was a start.
