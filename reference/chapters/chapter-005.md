# Chapter 5: The Airport

> POV: Carmen Soares | Style: literary

The hardest scene in Act 1 was not a scene at all.

Nothing dramatic happened. No one cried. No one changed their mind. No last-minute revelation, no running through the terminal, no emotional speeches. It was just a family, at an airport, on a Tuesday morning in June, boarding a plane to a country where none of them had ever been.

Carmen had expected something more. A sign, maybe. A feeling. The sense that the ground was shifting beneath her feet, that she was crossing a threshold into a different life. But the airport was just an airport. Fluorescent lights, linoleum floors, the smell of coffee and jet fuel and the particular dry chill of air conditioning trying to compensate for the summer heat outside.

They arrived two hours early, because Marta had planned it that way. Every detail of the morning had been planned. The bags were weighed and re-weighed. The passports were in Marta's purse, not Daniel's, not Carmen's, because Marta was the one who kept track of things. The boarding passes were already on her phone, screenshots saved in case the signal failed.

They stood in line at security. Carmen watched the people ahead of them — a family with a crying toddler, a businessman in a suit, an old woman traveling alone. They all looked ordinary. They all looked like they did this every day. She wondered if any of them were leaving for good, or if they were just visiting, or if they were coming home.

She wondered what it felt like to be coming home.

Her father put his hand on her shoulder. "You okay?"

She nodded. She wasn't sure if it was true, but she was tired of saying no.

---

Security was a process she had experienced before, but never as a participant. She had always been the one waving goodbye on the other side of the barrier. Now she was the one putting her backpack in a plastic bin, taking off her shoes, walking through the metal detector. The machine beeped — she had forgotten to take her hair clip out — and a uniformed woman waved her through with a patience that felt practiced.

She put her shoes back on. The laces were loose. She didn't bother tightening them.

The gate was at the end of a long hallway. They walked in silence. Her mother was carrying the heavier bag, the one with the laptops and the documents; Carmen could see the strain in her shoulders. Her father was carrying the smaller one, the one with their snacks and water bottles and a change of clothes in case the luggage was lost.

They sat down at the gate. The flight was not boarding yet. A full hour to wait.

Carmen sat between her parents and watched the planes through the window. They were big and gray and looked like they were made of something heavier than metal. She had flown before — once, to Florida, when she was seven. She remembered the feeling of takeoff, the press of the seat against her back, the way the world tilted and shrank. But that had been a vacation. A temporary thing. She had known, even then, that she would come back.

This time, she didn't know.

---

She thought about saying goodbye to Maya.

They had done it two days earlier, in Maya's backyard. It had been awkward — the kind of goodbye that neither of them knew how to do because they had never done one before. They had hugged, briefly, and Maya had said "text me," and Carmen had said "I will," and they both knew that the time difference and the distance and the sheer fact of living in different countries would slowly erode their friendship until it was just a memory.

It was the first time Carmen understood that friendships had lifespans. That some people existed in your life for a specific span of years and then the geography of your lives stopped overlapping and you became the person they used to know.

She wondered if her parents understood that too. If that was part of why they were leaving — because the geography of their lives had stopped overlapping with the country they lived in.

---

The boarding announcement came over the speakers.

Her mother stood up first. Her father followed. Carmen stayed in her seat for one more second, letting the announcement wash over her — "boarding group three, rows twenty through thirty" — before she stood up and picked up her backpack.

She followed her parents to the gate. The attendant scanned her boarding pass. The machine beeped. She walked down the jet bridge, the floor sloped slightly upward, the walls gray and windowless, and she thought: *this is it. This is the door.*

She stepped onto the plane.

The cabin was narrow and smelled of recycled air and cleaning products. They found their row: three seats, two on one side, one on the other. Her mother took the window. Her father took the aisle. Carmen took the middle seat, between them, the way she had when she was small enough to fall asleep across both their laps.

She buckled her seatbelt. The buckle clicked. A sound she had heard before, on the way to Florida.

This was not Florida.

---

The plane pushed back from the gate.

Carmen felt it — the slight jolt, the reversal of motion, the way the plane moved away from the terminal like it was backing out of a conversation. She watched through the window as the gate receded, as the people waiting at the next gate became small, as the whole terminal became a stripe of glass and steel.

The plane turned. The engines got louder. The seat vibrated beneath her.

Takeoff.

She pressed her head against the seat and felt the acceleration push her back, the way the plane gathered speed like it was making a decision. The runway markings blurred past. The wheels bumped once, twice, and then the bumping stopped, and the ground fell away.

She looked out the window.

The airport was getting smaller. The parking lots, the runways, the roads. She could see the city — the grid of streets, the clusters of buildings, the green patches of parks. She could see the highway that led to her school, the neighborhood where she had lived, the house that was no longer her house.

Higher. Everything was getting smaller. The cars were specks. The buildings were toys. The city was a pattern of shapes she was beginning to lose the ability to read.

Higher.

She watched the continent shrink.

---

She didn't cry.

She had thought she might. She had imagined this moment — the plane lifting off, the ground falling away, the tears coming — but they didn't come. She felt something, but it wasn't sadness. It was more like a pressure, a weight in her chest, the feeling of something being compressed into something smaller than itself.

She looked at the window. The land below was a green-brown blur. She could see the coast now — the line where the land met the water, the edge of the continent.

The edge.

She pressed her hand against the window. The plastic was cold. The sky was blue and empty.

"Look," her mother said.

Carmen turned. Her mother was pointing out the window, at the horizon, at the place where the edge of the continent was sliding away beneath them. The whole country was getting smaller and smaller and then it was gone, replaced by water, blue and infinite, stretching to the edge of the world.

"There it goes," her father said, quietly, more to himself than to them.

Carmen looked down. There was nothing below but ocean. The continent had disappeared.

She was not on the continent anymore.

---

The flight attendant came by with drinks. Her mother asked for water. Her father asked for coffee. Carmen asked for apple juice, because it was what she always asked for, and the flight attendant smiled and handed her a small plastic cup with foil over the top.

She drank the apple juice. It was cold and sweet.

She looked across the aisle. Her parents were holding hands over the armrest. Her mother's eyes were closed. Her father was looking out the window, at the water, at the sky, at whatever you looked at when you had left everything behind.

They were still holding hands. They had been holding hands since takeoff.

She tucked her legs up and leaned against the window and watched the clouds pass beneath the plane. They were white and dense, like cotton, like the diorama she had made for the science fair. The one about the water cycle. The one her father had said was the best one.

That felt like a long time ago.

She let her eyes close. The drone of the engines filled her ears. The seat vibrated gently. She was tired — more tired than she had realized, the kind of tired that came from weeks of holding something heavy and not knowing you were holding it.

She let herself drift.

The continent was gone. The life she had known was gone. The house, the school, the crack in the ceiling that looked like a rabbit, the sticker of the galaxy on her closet door — all of it was behind her, separated by an ocean she had only ever seen in pictures.

She was asleep before the meal cart came around.
