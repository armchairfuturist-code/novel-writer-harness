# Chapter 12: The Factions

**POV: Carmen Soares | November 2030 | Lisbon, Portugal**

---

The Attentionalist rally was held in a converted warehouse in Alcântara, the kind of space that had been industrial in a previous life and was now filled with exposed brick and carefully arranged chairs. Carmen arrived early, before the crowd, and stood near the back where she could watch without being watched.

The speaker was a man named Pedro Alves, a philosophy professor from Coimbra who had become one of Portugal's most prominent Attentionalists. He was tall and thin, with a beard that made him look like a prophet who had stumbled out of the nineteenth century and decided to stay. He spoke without notes, his hands moving in slow arcs.

"We are living through the greatest expansion of human consciousness since the development of language," he said. "The Mirror is not a threat to our humanity — it is the next step in its evolution. For the first time, we can see ourselves as we actually are, not as we imagine ourselves to be."

The audience applauded. Carmen did not.

She had been coming to these events for three weeks — Attentionalist rallies, Ascendant meetings, panel discussions, debate nights. She had watched the two movements form and harden, each one defining itself against the other. The Attentionalists believed that human consciousness was meant to be augmented, that the Mirror was a tool for self-understanding that would lead to a new era of empathy and connection. The Ascendants believed that consciousness was the last territory that belonged to humans alone, that the Mirror was an intrusion that would weaken the very thing it claimed to enhance.

She had read their manifestos. She had attended their meetings. She had listened to their arguments, their fears, their hopes.

And she still could not choose.

---

Two days later, she went to an Ascendant meeting in a church basement in Graça. The room was smaller, the chairs older, the lighting harsher. The speaker was a woman named Isabel, a former software engineer who had left her career to live in a community without digital connections.

"The Attentionalists will tell you that the Mirror is a tool," Isabel said. "They will tell you it is no different from a book, a telescope, a pair of glasses. But a book does not read you. A telescope does not watch you. The Mirror is not a tool — it is a relationship. And every relationship changes who you are."

A man in the audience raised his hand. "But what if the change is for the better?"

Isabel shook her head. "That is the question the Attentionalists never answer. Better by whose standards? Better for whom? The Mirror does not exist in a vacuum. It is built by corporations. It is funded by venture capital. It will be sold to the highest bidder. Do you trust those people to define what 'better' means?"

Carmen listened. She watched the faces in the room. Some were peaceful, she noticed. The Ascendants she had met carried a stillness that she recognized and envied — the stillness of people who had drawn a line and refused to cross it. But there was also something else. A defensiveness. A fear of the unknown that looked, from a certain angle, like the same fear that drove her mother.

She stayed until the end, then walked home through the narrow streets of Graça, the city lights flickering below her.

She felt split in two.

---

At home, the divide was no easier.

Her father had started using the Mirror. He had signed up for the beta program the day it opened and completed his first session three weeks ago. He did not talk about it in detail, but Carmen could see the change in him — a new calmness, a willingness to pause before speaking. He looked, she thought, like someone who had been given access to a part of himself he had not known was there.

"Dad," she asked one evening, "what did the Mirror show you?"

They were in the living room. Her father was reading a book — a history of the Portuguese empire that he had been working through for months. He marked his page and set it down.

"It showed me that I've been running toward the future my whole life because I was afraid of what I'd find if I stopped."

"What did you find when you stopped?"

"The thing I was running from wasn't as scary as the running itself."

She wanted to ask more. But she saw her mother pause in the hallway, and the moment closed.

---

Marta did not ask about the Mirror. She did not need to. Her silence on the subject was louder than any question.

Carmen found her one afternoon in the kitchen, chopping vegetables with the controlled precision of someone who needed her hands to be busy while her mind worked.

"Mom, can I ask you something?"

"You just did."

"Why won't you even try it?"

The knife stopped. Marta's hand rested on the blade for a moment, then resumed its work.

"Because I know what it's like to have your mind turned inside out by something you can't control. And I know that no amount of safety testing can prepare you for what that feels like."

"Is that what happened to you?"

Marta's jaw tightened. "That's not a conversation we're going to have."

"Why not?"

"Because some things are mine to carry and not yours to fix."

Carmen watched her mother's hands — the way they moved, the way they never stopped. She thought about the walls Marta had built, the fortress of vigilance and control that had kept her safe for so long.

"What if the Mirror isn't a threat?" Carmen asked. "What if it's a way to finally put some of those things down?"

Marta looked at her. For a moment, her face was unguarded. Then the mask slid back into place.

"I don't know how to put them down," she said. "And I don't trust a machine to teach me."

---

A friend chose a side. Leonor, who had sat next to Carmen in Portuguese class for two years, announced at lunch that she had become an Ascendant.

"I'm deleting my social media," she said. "I'm going to live without a smartphone for a year. My brother says the Mirror will be mandatory within a decade. I want to be clean before it arrives."

Rita stared at her. "Clean?"

"Of digital dependence. Of the need to be reflected by machines. I want to know who I am without it."

Carmen wanted to argue. She wanted to point out that Leonor was sixteen, that she had barely begun to figure out who she was with or without technology. But she recognized the conviction in Leonor's voice. She had heard it in her mother's voice. She had heard it at the Ascendant meetings.

Leonor had chosen. There was a clarity in that — a simplicity that Carmen envied even as she distrusted it.

"What about you?" Rita asked, turning to Carmen. "Attentionalist? Ascendant? Something else?"

"I don't know."

"You've been to every meeting."

"And I still don't know."

Rita shook her head. "How can you not know? It's the most important question there is."

Carmen looked at her friends — Leonor, who had found her answer in rejection; Rita, who had found hers in refusal. She thought about her father, who had found his in embrace. Her mother, who had found hers in fear.

"I don't know," she said again. "And I think pretending I do would be worse than not knowing."

---

That night, Carmen sat in her room with her laptop open to the Mirror beta registration page. She had completed six sessions now. Each one had shown her something she had not seen before.

She thought about the Ascendant meetings. The peace she had seen in their faces. The rejection of augmentation, the embrace of limits.

She thought about the Attentionalist rallies. The excitement, the sense of possibility, the belief that humanity was on the verge of something unprecedented.

She thought about her mother. Her father. Leonor. Rita.

She was the fulcrum. The point where both forces met. And she did not know which way she would fall.

She closed her laptop and lay in the dark, feeling the weight of the question pressing down on her.

She could not stay in the middle forever. The middle was not a place — it was a temporary suspension, a holding pattern that would eventually exhaust itself.

She would have to choose.

But not yet. Not tonight.

Tonight, she would let the question sit. She would let both sides speak. She would trust that when the time came, she would know which voice was hers.
