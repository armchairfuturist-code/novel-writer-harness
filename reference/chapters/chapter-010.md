# Chapter 10: The Announcement

**POV: Carmen Soares | March 2030 | Lisbon, Portugal**

---

The announcement came on a Tuesday.

Carmen was in her room, half-watching a Portuguese drama on her laptop while scrolling through her phone, when the notification appeared. It was a news alert, and she almost swiped it away — she had trained her thumb to dismiss notifications without reading them, a habit borrowed from her mother, who treated every ping as a potential intrusion. But something made her pause. The headline was longer than usual.

*Mirror AI Announces Digital Consciousness Transfer: "We Have Solved the Hard Problem," CEO Claims.*

She read it twice. The words did not rearrange themselves into sense the second time.

She clicked.

The article was long. She read it standing up, her phone held in both hands, the Portuguese drama playing unwatched behind her. The CEO of Mirror AI, a woman named Dr. Elena Vasquez, had held a press conference in San Francisco. She had stood on a stage with a screen behind her displaying a blue-and-white diagram of a brain. She had said:

*"We have created a digital reflection of human consciousness. Not a simulation. Not a chatbot. A reflection. It learns you the way you learn yourself — by watching, by listening, by being present. It does not replace you. It mirrors you. And in that mirror, you can see what you could not see before."*

Carmen scrolled. The comments were already multiplying. The share count was in the hundreds of thousands. The video of the press conference had four million views.

She watched it. Dr. Vasquez was in her forties, with silver-streaked hair and the kind of calm that suggested she had been expecting the world's reaction. She answered questions from journalists with patience and precision. No, it was not a threat to privacy. Yes, it was entirely voluntary. No, they were not releasing it to the public yet — beta testing would begin in six months, limited to five thousand participants.

*"This is the most important technology in human history,"* Dr. Vasquez said. *"More important than fire. More important than writing. More important than the internet. Because for the first time, we can see ourselves as we actually are."*

Carmen put her phone down.

She sat on her bed and stared at the wall.

She thought about the translation earbuds. The hollow feeling. The invisible effort.

This was different. This was not translation. This was something else entirely.

---

Dinner that evening was supposed to be normal. Marta had made bacalhau — a recipe she had learned from a neighbor, one of the few Portuguese dishes she had mastered. The table was set. The wine was poured. Daniel was telling a story about a client who had tried to pay him in cryptocurrency that had since collapsed.

Carmen listened. Nodded. Forked fish into her mouth and chewed without tasting.

She was waiting. She could feel the shape of the conversation coming, the way you can feel a storm in the pressure change before the first drop falls.

Her father mentioned it first.

"Did you see the Mirror announcement?" he asked, his tone casual, as if he were asking whether anyone had checked the weather.

Marta's fork paused mid-air. A fraction of a second.

"I saw it," she said.

"What do you think?"

"I think it's a lot of claims without evidence."

"The New York Times verified the demo. They had independent neuroscientists observe."

Marta set her fork down. She did it carefully, deliberately — a woman controlling her movements because she could not control the conversation. "Observing a demo is not the same as understanding what they actually built. You know how many times tech companies have announced consciousness this decade?"

"This is different."

"Every time is different. Until it isn't."

Carmen watched them. She had seen this dynamic before — her father's openness to possibility, her mother's skepticism-as-armor. But there was something else tonight. A sharpness to Marta's voice that went beyond caution.

"What's the Mirror, exactly?" Carmen asked.

Both parents turned to look at her. She had interrupted a conversation they had been having without her, and for a moment she saw them remember she was there.

Daniel leaned forward. "It's a technology that creates a digital model of your consciousness. Your memories, your thought patterns, your blind spots. It shows you a reflection of yourself that you can interact with."

"Like a clone?"

"More like a mirror that talks back."

Carmen turned to her mother. "And what do you think it is?"

Marta was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice was lower than before. "I think it's an invasion. I think giving a machine access to your inner life is the most dangerous thing a person can do. And I think the people building it are so busy asking whether they can that they haven't stopped to ask whether they should."

"The same argument could have been made about the internet," Daniel said.

"The same argument *was* made about the internet. And look where we are."

"Where we are is complicated. That doesn't mean we shouldn't have built it."

"It means we should have built it more carefully."

"And who decides what careful enough looks like?"

They were not arguing. They were speaking in the measured tones of people who had learned to disagree without raising their voices. But the disagreement was real. Carmen could feel it in the space between them, a tension that had nothing to do with the food on the table.

---

After dinner, Carmen found her father in the study. He was at his desk, the blue light of his monitor reflecting off his glasses, reading something she couldn't see.

"Can I ask you something?"

He turned. "Always."

"Do you really think the Mirror is a good thing?"

He took his glasses off. Rubbed his eyes. When he looked at her, his face was open in a way it rarely was — the guard down, the futurist's certainty set aside.

"I think it's a door," he said. "And I've spent my life believing that doors should be opened, not locked. But I also know that not every door leads somewhere good."

"Then why do you want to open it?"

"Because I'd rather see what's on the other side than spend my life wondering."

She thought about this. It was the most honest answer he could have given, and she recognized it as the same impulse that had brought them to Portugal. The willingness to step through. The refusal to stay in a room that felt smaller than it should.

"Mom thinks it's dangerous."

"Your mother has good reasons to be afraid of things that get inside." He said it gently, without judgment. "She's spent her whole life building walls. The Mirror looks like a door in those walls. And she's spent too long building them to watch someone knock one down."

---

Carmen found her mother in the kitchen, washing dishes by hand even though the dishwasher was empty. The repetitive motion of it — the water, the sponge, the circle of the plate — seemed to be holding her in place.

"Why don't you trust it?" Carmen asked.

Marta did not turn around. "Trust what?"

"The Mirror."

The sponge stopped moving. Marta held it against the plate for a long moment, then set both down in the sink and turned to face her daughter.

"Because I know what happens when you let something into your head that you can't control." Her voice was flat. "I know what it costs to have your thoughts reflected back at you by something that doesn't have your interests at heart."

"Dad says it's a door."

"Your father sees doors everywhere. He sees doors in walls that are there for a reason."

"Are the walls there for a reason?"

Marta looked at her. For a moment, Carmen saw something raw in her mother's face — a fear so old and so deep that it looked like a geological formation, something that had been pressed into shape over decades.

"Yes," Marta said. "The walls are there for a reason."

She turned back to the sink. The water ran. The sponge resumed its circles.

Carmen stood in the doorway and watched her mother wash dishes that were already clean, and she understood, for the first time, that her parents were not just disagreeing about a technology. They were disagreeing about the nature of safety. About whether the world was something you protected yourself from or something you walked into.

She did not know which one was right.

She suspected, with the quiet certainty of a child who had learned to see both sides, that they were both right and both wrong, and that the truth was somewhere in the gap between them.

---

That night, she lay in bed and searched for the Mirror on her phone. She found the press conference video again. She watched Dr. Vasquez's calm face, listened to her words about reflection and self-knowledge, and felt something stir in her chest. Not fear. Not excitement. Something between the two. A curiosity that felt like hunger.

She looked at the comments. They were divided, fiercely so.

*This is the end of privacy as we know it.*

*This is the beginning of real self-understanding.*

*The hubris is staggering.*

*The potential is limitless.*

She scrolled until her eyes hurt, then put the phone down and stared at the ceiling.

She could hear the house settling around her. The creak of floorboards. The low hum of the refrigerator. Somewhere in the other room, her parents were talking in voices too low to make out the words.

She thought about doors. She thought about walls.

She did not know which one she would choose when the time came.

But she knew, with the same certainty that had carried her through four years of learning a new language, that the choice was coming. And that she would have to make it alone.

---

In the morning, her father was already gone. Her mother was at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee that had gone cold, staring at her phone.

"It's on every news site," Marta said, without looking up. "They're calling it the most important invention since the printing press."

"What do you call it?"

Marta was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "I call it a test. Of whether we've learned anything."

She stood up, poured her cold coffee down the sink, and left the room.

Carmen sat at the table in the empty kitchen and pulled out her own phone. She watched the press conference again. She watched Dr. Vasquez's steady eyes, heard the conviction in her voice. She thought about her father's curiosity and her mother's fear, and she felt both of them moving inside her, two currents in the same river.

She did not know yet that this technology would remake her life. That she would try it, and be changed by it, and spend years trying to understand what the change meant. That it would fracture her family and show her parts of herself she had never seen.

She only knew that something had begun. The announcement on a Tuesday. The dinner table argument. The two poles of her parents' beliefs, pulling her in opposite directions.

She closed her phone and got up to get ready for school.

The world had changed again. She was learning to recognize the feeling.
