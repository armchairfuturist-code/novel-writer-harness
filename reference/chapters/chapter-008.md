# Chapter 8: The New Normal

> POV: Daniel Soares | Style: literary

Three months in, Daniel found a rhythm.

It wasn't the rhythm he had known — the 8 AM commute, the office coffee, the meetings that could have been emails, the drive home in traffic. That rhythm was gone, buried somewhere over the Atlantic. The new rhythm was different. Slower. More uncertain. He was learning to live in a key he didn't know how to play.

He found the job through a former colleague who had gone remote before it was fashionable. A startup based in Berlin, building something in the AI ethics space — the same kind of work he had done before, but in a different language, on a different continent. The hours were strange; the morning standup was at 3 PM Lisbon time, which meant he spent his mornings free and his afternoons in front of a laptop. It paid less than his old job. It was enough.

He set up his workspace at the small table in the living room, the one that had been covered in visa spreadsheets a few months earlier. The spreadsheets were gone now. In their place were a laptop, a notebook, a coffee mug that said *Lisboa* in blue letters, bought from a souvenir shop near the waterfront. He had become a man who worked from a table in a living room in a country he had never planned to live in.

He was learning to accept strangeness as a permanent condition.

---

Marta found contract work through João's network.

She was doing systems architecture for a Portuguese tech company that needed someone to redesign their cloud infrastructure. The work was good, and she was good at it, and Daniel watched her disappear into it the way she disappeared into a spreadsheet — with the focus of someone who needed structure to keep the chaos at bay.

She was better at this than he was. The transition. The adaptation. She had done it before, he reminded himself, even though she never talked about it. She had left her father's house at seventeen and built a life from nothing. She knew how to start over.

He watched her on the balcony sometimes, laptop open, a cigarette she didn't really smoke burning in her hand. She looked out at the city the way she had looked at the visa spreadsheets — not with fear, but with calculation. She was figuring out how to make this place hers.

He loved her for that. He loved her for the way she never stopped moving forward, even when she didn't know where forward was.

---

Carmen made a friend.

Her name was Sofia, and she appeared in their lives gradually — first as a name Carmen mentioned at dinner, then as a voice on the phone speaking a mix of Portuguese and English, then as a physical presence at their front door, invited over on a Saturday afternoon.

Sofia was smaller than Carmen, with dark hair and a serious face that broke into sudden smiles. She spoke English with a heavy accent and Portuguese with the speed of someone who had never had to learn it. She and Carmen communicated in a language of their own — half English, half Portuguese, full understanding.

Daniel watched them play in the small living room, watched them run out onto the balcony, watched them huddle over Carmen's phone watching videos. He watched his daughter laugh in a language that was not her first, and he felt something loosen in his chest.

She was going to be okay. Not today, not tomorrow. But she was going to be okay.

---

The video call with family back home happened on a Sunday.

Daniel's sister, Emily, lived in Chicago with her husband and two kids. They hadn't seen each other since the Christmas before last, and the distance had grown wider than miles. The video call was supposed to bridge it, but video calls never bridged anything. They just made the distance visible.

"Is it amazing?" Emily asked, her face on the screen, pixelated and slightly lagging. "Portugal?"

"It's different," Daniel said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

They talked for an hour. Emily told him about the kids, about work, about the weather. She didn't ask the questions he could see in her eyes — *when are you coming back?* — and he was grateful for that. He didn't have an answer.

Before she hung up, she said: "You're brave, you know. For doing this."

Daniel looked at the wall behind her. The familiar kitchen. The refrigerator covered in the kids' drawings. The life he had walked away from.

"I don't feel brave," he said.

"Nobody does."

The call ended. The screen went dark. Daniel sat in the quiet of the apartment, the Lisbon afternoon light slanting through the window, and tried to feel brave. He couldn't. He just felt tired.

---

The news from America got worse.

He didn't seek it out. It found him, the way bad news always does — headlines in his feed, notifications from apps he had forgotten to mute, conversations with colleagues who still lived there. The country was not getting better. The barricades around the Capitol had become permanent. The graphs with the lines going up and to the right continued their ascent.

He stopped watching the news. He had left partly to stop living inside the noise, and he was determined to honor that, even when the noise followed him.

But he couldn't stop thinking about it. The school hallway. The security camera. The thought that had cracked everything open. He had left the country, but the thought hadn't left him. It was still there, waiting, in the quiet moments when he wasn't paying attention.

He wondered if it would ever leave. He wondered if he had been carrying it for so long that it had become part of his skeleton.

---

One evening, he found himself on the balcony alone.

The sun was setting over the city, the light turning gold and then orange and then a deep, dusty pink. The rooftops were a mosaic of red tile and white walls, laundry lines and satellite dishes, the ordinary infrastructure of ordinary lives. He watched the light change and thought about how many sunsets he had missed in America, looking at screens, thinking about the future, not realizing that the present was slipping past him.

The door opened behind him. Marta came out, holding two glasses of wine.

"Carmen's asleep," she said.

She handed him a glass and sat down in the chair beside him. They drank in silence, watching the sky darken.

"Do you ever regret it?" he asked.

"Regret what?"

"Leaving."

She was quiet for a long time. The sun had almost disappeared now, just a thin line of orange at the edge of the world.

"Every day," she said. "And also never."

He looked at her. Her face was half in shadow, half in the last of the light. She looked older than she had looked in Michigan. Or maybe she looked more like herself. He wasn't sure anymore.

"That's a terrible answer," he said.

"It's the only one I have."

He laughed. It was a quiet laugh, barely a laugh at all. But it was real.

"Okay," he said. "That's fair."

They finished their wine as the city darkened. The first stars appeared. The church bells that had woken them on their first morning were silent now, but he could hear music from somewhere — a radio, a party, someone living their life in the same city where he was learning to live his.

---

The fragile rhythm held.

Mornings, Daniel made coffee and sat at the small table while the sun came up. The apartment was quiet, the way it got before the city fully woke, and he used that time to read, to think, to be still. Afternoons, he worked. Evenings, they ate together — sometimes at home, sometimes at the restaurant around the corner where the owner had started to recognize them and would bring *pastéis de nata* without being asked.

Carmen was learning Portuguese faster than either of them. She came home from school with new words, new phrases, new pieces of the language she was assembling like a puzzle. She had started correcting them, gently, the way children do when they discover they know something their parents don't.

They had a routine. It was not the routine they had left behind. It was thinner, more provisional, like a path that had been walked just enough to be visible but not enough to be permanent.

But it was theirs.

---

On a Tuesday afternoon in September, Daniel walked Carmen to school.

The walk took fifteen minutes. They passed the bakery where Carmen had smiled at the woman behind the counter — now she could order in Portuguese without hesitating. They passed the community center where the Portuguese classes were held — now they only went once a week, because Carmen had advanced past the beginner level and Daniel and Marta were keeping up as best they could.

They reached the school gate. Carmen stopped.

"Pai," she said. It was the first time she had called him that.

He looked at her. It was the Portuguese word for *father*, but she had never used it before. She had always said *dad* or *papa*, the English words, the familiar words. But now she was learning the new words, and she was trying them on, the way you try on a coat that doesn't quite fit yet but will, in time.

"Yes?"

She didn't say anything else. She just looked at him, and he looked at her, and they stood at the gate of a school in a country neither of them had been born in, speaking a language that was becoming theirs.

"You're doing good, mija," he said. "You're doing so good."

She nodded. Then she turned and walked through the gate, joining the stream of children in uniforms, becoming part of the current of a life that was still new but was starting to feel like something other than temporary.

Daniel stood at the gate for a long time after she disappeared inside.

The bells rang. The doors closed. The street was quiet.

He turned and walked home, past the bakery, past the community center, past the small apartment with the balcony where his wife was probably already at the kitchen table, working on systems architecture for a country she had never planned to live in.

They were building something. He couldn't see the shape of it yet. But he could feel it, the way you feel the shape of a room in the dark.

He walked home.

The sun was warm on his face. The city hummed around him. He was a man without a country, living in a country that was slowly becoming his. He didn't know if they would stay. He didn't know if they would go back. He didn't know if any of it had been the right choice.

But his daughter had called him *Pai*, and he had watched her walk into a school where she had friends, and the news from America was not playing on his television, and for this moment — this one, ordinary moment — that was enough.

The fragile rhythm held.

For now.
