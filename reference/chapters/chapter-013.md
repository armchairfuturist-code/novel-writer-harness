# Chapter 13: The Ascendant

**POV: Carmen Soares | January 2031 | Sintra, Portugal**

---

The Ascendant community was called Terra Nova, and it was hidden in the hills outside Sintra, accessible by a dirt road that looked like it had been carved by mules. Carmen had been invited by a woman named Helena, whom she had met at the Graça meeting. Helena was thirty-two, a former graphic designer who had left Lisbon three years ago to live without digital technology.

"You do know I have a phone, right?" Carmen had said, when Helena handed her the handwritten directions.

"I know you have a lot of things," Helena had replied. "The question is whether they have you."

The bus dropped Carmen at a crossroads. She walked the last two kilometers, her backpack growing heavier with each step. The January air was cold and clean. The city sounds had faded into birdsong and wind.

Terra Nova appeared between the trees: a cluster of stone buildings around a central courtyard, a garden that had been prepared for winter, a greenhouse with light glinting off its roof. There were maybe thirty people living here. They moved slowly, deliberately, as if time were a resource they had learned to use differently.

Helena met her at the gate. She was taller than Carmen remembered, her hair pulled back in a practical braid, her face bare of makeup. She wore wool pants and a thick sweater, and she moved with the economy of someone who spent her days doing physical work.

"You came," Helena said. It was not a question.

"I said I would."

"Come. I'll show you."

---

The tour took an hour. The gardens, the kitchen, the common house with its high ceilings and wood-burning stove. The workshop where they repaired tools. The library — a room of floor-to-ceiling shelves filled with books, no screens anywhere.

"We have a computer," Helena said, anticipating Carmen's question. "In the office. For administrative things. Banking, legal documents. We share it. Two hours a week, by rota."

"No phones?"

"No phones. No internet. No social media. No news." She said it without pride, without judgment. A statement of fact.

"How do you know what's happening in the world?"

"We don't. Not in the way you mean. We hear things from visitors. We learn what matters. The rest is noise."

Carmen thought about the constant stream of notifications, headlines, updates. The noise that filled every quiet moment. The silence of this place was so complete it felt like a sound in itself.

They walked into the courtyard. A man was splitting wood. A woman was mending a fence. The work was physical, visible, completeable. There was something satisfying about watching it.

"What brought you here?" Carmen asked.

Helena was quiet for a moment. "I worked in tech. Seven years. I was good at it. I helped build recommendation algorithms that learned people's preferences better than they knew themselves. And one day I realized I had spent a decade teaching machines to understand humans without understanding myself."

"And this is better?"

"This is harder. And yes, it's better."

---

Lunch was vegetable stew and bread baked that morning. The food was simple and tasted like effort. Carmen sat at a long table with a dozen people and listened to them talk.

They talked about the weather. About a broken fence that needed mending. About whether to plant beans or tomatoes in the spring. The conversation moved at a different pace than she was used to — slower, more careful. People finished their sentences. They listened to each other without checking phones.

She found herself relaxing into it. The absence of notifications had created a kind of spaciousness in her attention that she had not known she was missing.

After lunch, Helena's friend Miguel joined them. He was in his forties, with calloused hands and a calm that seemed to radiate from him. He had been at Terra Nova for five years.

"What do you think of our community?" he asked.

"It's peaceful."

"It's meant to be. But peace is not the same as safety."

"What do you mean?"

He leaned back in his chair, looking out at the hills. "There are people who come here looking for an escape. A way to opt out of a world they don't like. That's not what this is. This is a choice to live within limits. To accept that a bounded life is a meaningful life. The Attentionalists believe that more is always better — more awareness, more data, more connection. We believe that more is often just more."

"But isn't it a form of fear?" Carmen asked. "Choosing limits because you're afraid of what lies beyond them?"

Miguel smiled. "Good question. And yes, for some people, it is. The question you have to ask yourself is: what is my reason? Am I choosing limits because I'm afraid, or because I've seen the alternative and decided it's not for me?"

"And what's your reason?"

"I've seen the alternative. I worked in advertising for fifteen years. I spent a decade learning how to manipulate people's attention for profit. The Mirror is the same thing, just deeper. It claims to be about self-knowledge, but it's owned by the same people who brought us targeted advertising. I don't trust them with my inner life."

"So you reject it entirely?"

"I reject the premise that technology is the solution to problems created by technology. The Ascendant path is not about rejecting the modern world — it's about remembering that the modern world is not the only world."

---

Carmen stayed until dusk. She helped wash dishes. She sat in the library and read a physical book about beekeeping. She watched the light change over the hills.

She felt the appeal of this life. The discipline of it. The peace of bounded choices. The refusal to surrender what made you human to a machine that could never understand you.

But she also felt the judgment. The rigidity underneath the calm. The suspicion of anyone who chose differently. The fear that drove the purity.

She thought about the Mirror sessions she had done. The things she had learned. The blind spots that had been illuminated. She could not pretend that those sessions had been meaningless. They had shown her parts of herself she had never seen.

"Can I ask you something?" she said to Helena, as they stood at the gate.

"Of course."

"Do you ever wonder what the Mirror would show you?"

Helena was quiet for a long time. The wind moved through the trees. Somewhere, a bird called out.

"Every day," she said. "But that's not the same as wanting to know."

---

The bus ride back to Lisbon was dark and cold. Carmen pressed her face against the window and watched the lights of the city spread out below her.

She had seen the appeal of the Ascendant path. She had felt the peace of a bounded life. She had respected the discipline, the intentionality, the refusal to surrender what made them human.

But she had also seen the fear behind the purity. The judgment hidden in the peace. The walls that kept things out and also kept them in.

She thought about her mother, who had built her life around walls.

She thought about her father, who had walked through doors.

She thought about herself, standing at the gate of a community that had found its answer, and still not knowing her own.

The city lights grew brighter as the bus descended. Carmen watched them approach, carrying both worlds inside her, not yet ready to choose one.

She was beginning to understand that choosing was not the only work. The work was also in the not-choosing. In the staying-open. In the refusal to close herself off from either voice until she knew, truly knew, which one was hers.
