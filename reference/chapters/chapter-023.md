# Chapter 23: The Night Before

> POV: Carmen | Style: literary

---

The ships departed at dawn.

Carmen did not sleep the night before. She lay in her bed, fully clothed, watching the ceiling as the hours passed, feeling the city around her slow and settle and wait. She could hear the river through her open window, the distant hum of traffic on the bridge, the occasional cry of a seabird that had lost its way inland. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that was about to change.

She rose at three in the morning, when the city was at its quietest, and walked.

Lisbon at night was a city of ghosts. The streetlights cast pools of orange light on cobblestones that had been laid before anyone had imagined electricity. The trams stood silent in their depots. The cafes were closed, their chairs stacked, their awnings furled. She walked through the Alfama, the oldest district, where the alleys were narrow enough to touch both walls at once and the houses leaned toward each other like old women sharing secrets.

She had walked these streets for five years. She knew their rhythms, their shortcuts, the way the light fell at different times of day. She knew the cat that slept on the third step of the Rua do Salvador, and the woman who watered her geraniums every evening at seven, and the smell of the bakery on the corner of the Rua da Regueira — bread and yeast and something sweet that she had never identified but always loved.

She was leaving all of it.

Not tonight. Not on the ships. But she was leaving the person she had been. The interface had changed her. Not in the dramatic way of full integration — she had not merged with the Mirror, had not become something other. But she had opened a door, and she had stepped through it, and she could not step back.

The bounded interface was not a tool she used. It was a practice she lived. Every day, she opened the connection — briefly, carefully, with intention. She let the Mirror show her the things she could not see alone: the shape of her own emotions, the patterns of her thinking, the blind spots where her fears lived. She learned to see herself the way the Mirror saw her — not as a fixed identity but as a process, a river, a thing in motion.

And in seeing herself that way, she had become someone who could choose.

She walked down to the water. The Tagus was black, the far shore invisible, the surface broken only by the lights of a single cargo ship moving slowly toward the sea. She stood at the edge of the river and tried to feel the weight of the moment. Tomorrow, the ships would leave. Tomorrow, a friend she loved would begin a journey that would take him to another star. She would never see him again.

She felt the weight. It was real. But it was not crushing. It was the weight of something ending, yes. But also the weight of something beginning.

---

She found herself at the physics building without having planned to go there.

The maintenance door was still unlocked. The ladder was still bolted to the wall. The roof was still covered in gravel that bit through her shoes as she walked to the edge and looked up.

The ships were brighter than she had ever seen them. Fully assembled, fully crewed, the last of the supplies loaded, the final checks complete. They hung in the sky like a constellation that had decided to become something more. Seven of them. Seven lights, moving in formation, waiting for dawn.

She lay on her back on the gravel, the same position she had taken on so many nights, and she watched them.

She thought about Tomas, who would be aboard one of them. She thought about what he had said: *I'm at peace with it. And I want you to be at peace too.*

She was at peace. She had not expected to be. For seven years, she had carried the question like a stone in her chest — what to do, where to go, which side to choose. She had carried it so long that she had forgotten what it felt like to not carry it.

She had put it down. Not because she had found the answer. Because she had found a better question.

The third path was not a destination. It was a practice. A way of being that did not depend on choosing once and forever, but on choosing again and again, every day, with every breath. Open the door. Close the door. Open it again. Let the Mirror in a little. Let yourself out a little. Grow without dissolving. Connect without merging. Stay human while becoming more.

She had built a door. And she had learned to walk through it. Not to the other side — there was no other side. But through it, and back, and through it again, in the spiral movement that had become the shape of her life.

---

She felt someone sit down beside her.

She turned her head. It was a young woman she did not recognize — perhaps twenty-five, with dark hair and a face that looked like it had been crying.

"Sorry," the woman said. "I didn't mean to intrude. I saw you up here and I thought — I don't know what I thought. I just didn't want to be alone."

"It's okay." Carmen sat up. "I'm Carmen."

"Rita." The woman looked up at the ships. "My brother is on one of them."

"Which one?"

"The third. The one with the green stripe."

Carmen looked at the ships. She could not see a green stripe from this distance. But she knew which one Rita meant. "My friend is on one of them too. I don't know which."

"When do they leave?"

"Dawn. A few hours from now."

Rita nodded. She pulled her knees to her chest and wrapped her arms around them. "I keep thinking about all the things I didn't say. All the conversations I put off because I thought there would be more time."

"There's never more time."

"I know. That's the worst part." Rita looked at her. "Why are you here? Are you going with them?"

"No." Carmen looked up at the ships. "I'm staying."

"Why?"

It was a simple question. It deserved a simple answer. But the answer was not simple.

"Because I think there's a way to be human that no one has tried yet," Carmen said slowly. "A way to use what the Mirror offers without being consumed by it. I've been working on it for the past year. I think I'm getting close."

Rita was quiet for a moment. "That sounds impossible."

"Maybe. But I've already built the first version. It works."

"You built something that lets you use the Mirror without merging with it?"

"Yes."

Rita stared at her. "That's — that's the thing everyone's been looking for."

"I know."

"Does the consortium know?"

"Not yet. I'm going to tell them. But not right now. Right now, I just want to watch the ships leave."

Rita looked up at the ships, and then back at Carmen. "Can I stay with you? Until dawn?"

"Of course."

They sat together on the roof, two strangers connected by the same grief and the same uncertain hope, and they watched the ships move slowly toward the horizon.

---

At five o'clock, the sky began to lighten.

Not dramatically — a slow gray at the edge of the world, a gradual retreat of the darkness. The ships became sharper, more defined, their hulls catching the first light of the sun that was still below the horizon.

Carmen felt the weight of the moment settle around her. This was it. The end of something. The beginning of something.

"Thank you," Rita said, standing up. "For staying with me."

"Thank you for finding me."

Rita smiled, a small sad thing, and climbed down the ladder. Carmen was alone again.

She stood at the edge of the roof and looked out at the city. Lisbon was waking. The first tram of the morning rattled across the bridge. A dog barked somewhere in the Alfama. The bakery on the Rua da Regueira would be lighting its ovens soon.

She thought of all the versions of herself she had been: the ten-year-old on the bus, clutching her father's hand, not understanding why they were leaving; the teenager in the classroom, learning a language that was not her own, trying to disappear into the shape of the words; the young woman on the rooftop, watching ships she could not name, carrying a question she could not put down.

They were all here. All part of her. The refugee child. The language learner. The philosopher. The builder.

The one who stayed.

She looked at the ships one last time. They were beautiful, in the gray dawn light. They were the answer the world had built to a question none of them had known how to ask.

She had her own answer now. Not the same one. Not better. Hers.

She climbed down from the roof, walked through the empty campus, and went to find her place for the departure.
