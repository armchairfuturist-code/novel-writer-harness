# Chapter 4: The Last Month

> POV: Daniel Soares | Style: literary

The For Sale sign went up on a Tuesday.

Daniel watched the realtor hammer it into the front lawn. The thud of the mallet traveled through the ground and up through his feet, and he stood at the living room window and felt like he was watching someone drive a stake into something alive. The house had been in his name for twelve years. Twelve years of mortgage payments, roof repairs, the slow accumulation of a life. Now it was a listing on Zillow with a price tag that made him feel like he was selling a piece of himself.

He quit his job on Thursday.

The conversation was short. His manager was a woman named Priya who had been kind to him for four years, and she listened to his explanation without interrupting. When he finished, she said: "Are you sure?" And he said: "No. But I'm doing it anyway."

He cleared his desk that afternoon. There wasn't much — a framed photo of Carmen and Marta, a notebook filled with meeting notes he would never need again, a coffee mug with a chip in the rim. He put them in a cardboard box and carried it through the office for the last time. People looked at him. He didn't know what they saw.

The parking lot was hot and empty. He put the box in the trunk of the Camry and sat in the driver's seat for a long time without starting the engine.

He had worked since he was twenty-two. He had never not had a job. The absence of it felt like a hole in his chest, a space where something familiar had been removed. He didn't know yet what would fill it.

---

The friend who said he was overreacting was named Kevin.

They had known each other since college — not close friends, but the kind who stayed in touch through annual Christmas cards and the occasional beer when their schedules aligned. Kevin worked in finance. Kevin had a wife and two kids and a house in the suburbs and a worldview that could be summarized as: *things aren't that bad, people just like to complain.*

He came over on a Saturday afternoon. Daniel hadn't invited him; Kevin had heard through someone else and showed up at the door with a six-pack of beer and the expression of a man who was about to deliver a lecture.

"You can't be serious," Kevin said, settling onto the porch swing. "Portugal? You're moving to Portugal?"

"We're trying to."

"Because of the school thing?"

"Because of a lot of things."

Kevin shook his head. He popped open a beer and took a long drink. "Look, I get it. The news is scary. It's always been scary. But you don't just *leave*."

"Why not?"

"Because this is your home. This is where your life is."

Daniel looked at the street. The Camry was parked at the curb. The grass needed mowing. A kid on a bike rode past, the same kid who rode past every Saturday. The same street, the same houses, the same everything.

"It doesn't feel like home anymore," Daniel said quietly.

Kevin was quiet for a moment. Then he said: "You're making a mistake."

"Maybe."

"I mean it. You're going to regret this."

Daniel looked at his old friend. He wanted to explain — the school hallway, the fluorescent lights, the thought that had cracked something open inside him. He wanted to say: *I don't want to spend the rest of my life normalizing fear.* But he knew Kevin wouldn't understand. Kevin was still living in the country that was getting sicker, and he hadn't noticed yet, or he had noticed and decided it was easier to stay.

"Maybe I will," Daniel said. "But I'd rather regret leaving than regret staying."

Kevin finished his beer in silence. He left the empty bottle on the porch railing when he went.

---

The visa anxiety lived in his chest like a second heartbeat.

It was there when he woke up, there when he tried to sleep, there in the moments between. The waiting was the worst part. The application had been submitted, the fees paid, the biometric appointment completed. Now they waited. Days turned into weeks. The consulate website said *processing time: 8-12 weeks*, and every day without an email was a day of not knowing whether they would be allowed to leave or forced to stay.

The lawyers' fees were a separate wound. Marta had found a immigration attorney who specialized in Portuguese visas, and the consultation alone cost eight hundred dollars. The full representation was five thousand. They paid it because they had no choice, but the money coming out of their savings account was a physical sensation — a subtraction that brought them closer to zero with every check they wrote.

At night, Daniel lay awake and calculated. Savings. House sale proceeds. The cost of moving. The cost of setting up a life in a country where they had no credit history, no network, no safety net. The numbers never quite added up to enough. They added up to *we'll figure it out*, which was not a plan but a prayer.

---

He took Carmen to the park on the last Saturday.

Not the big park with the soccer fields and the playground equipment. The small one, around the corner from their house, where she had learned to ride a bike. The parking lot was empty. The grass was overgrown. The bike path was cracked in places, the asphalt pushed up by tree roots heaving beneath the surface.

He didn't tell her why they were there. He just said: "Let's go for a walk."

They walked the path in silence. The trees were full and green, the way they got in late spring, and the light filtered through the leaves in shifting patterns. Carmen walked ahead of him, her steps light, her eyes scanning the trees for squirrels.

She had learned to ride a bike on this path. He remembered the day — her fifth birthday, the pink bike with training wheels, the way she had wobbled and fallen and gotten up and fallen again. He remembered running behind her, one hand on the seat, feeling her balance click into place. *I'm doing it!* she had shouted. And then she had turned around and seen him ten feet behind her, not holding the seat anymore, and she had fallen again, but she was laughing.

He wondered if she remembered that day. He wondered if she would remember this one.

They reached the end of the path and turned around.

"Are you scared?" Carmen asked.

"Yes," he said.

"Me too."

He looked at her. She was walking with her hands in her pockets, her hair falling across her face. She was ten years old, and she was about to lose everything she knew, and she was walking beside him without complaint.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"For what?"

"For making you leave."

She stopped walking. She looked up at him, and her eyes were the same eyes that had looked at him from the back seat of the Camry on the night of the school event, asking if he was okay.

"I heard you," she said. "That night. You told Mami you didn't want to think it anymore."

He didn't know what to say.

"I think that's okay," she said. "Not wanting to think it anymore."

She started walking again, and he followed.

---

The boxes started accumulating in the garage.

Packing was a strange kind of grief. Every object required a decision: keep, sell, donate, throw away. Each decision was a small death, a severing of the connection between the thing and the life it had been part of. The coffee table they had bought at a flea market when they were young and broke. The bookshelf Daniel had built with his own hands. The rocking chair Marta had used when Carmen was a baby.

They sold most of it. The buyer came on a Wednesday, a young couple with a truck and a flatbed trailer. They walked through the house and pointed at things and Daniel said "yes" or "no" or "that's not for sale" in a voice that sounded like it belonged to someone else.

When they left, the house felt hollow. The furniture was gone. The walls were bare. The rooms echoed when you spoke.

Carmen stood in the middle of the living room, in the empty space where the couch used to be.

"It looks smaller," she said.

"It is smaller," Daniel said. "Without the things in it."

"Are we going to be smaller too?"

He looked at her. The question was innocent — she was asking about luggage, about space, about the logistics of fitting a life into suitcases. But it landed differently. It landed like a weight he hadn't known he was carrying.

"No," he said. "We're not going to be smaller."

He didn't know if it was true. He hoped it was.

---

The night before the flight, he couldn't sleep.

He lay in bed next to Marta, listening to her breathe. She was asleep — finally, after weeks of the same insomnia that had kept her at the kitchen table with spreadsheets at 2 AM. He was grateful she was resting. He didn't want to wake her.

He slipped out of bed and walked through the house.

It was empty now. The echoes of their life were gone. The walls were bare. The kitchen counter had a single mug on it — the one with the chip in the rim, from his office. He had kept it. He didn't know why.

He stood in the kitchen and looked at the place where the table had been. The table where they had made the decision. The table where Marta had cried. The table where Carmen had asked if they were coming back.

He had been born in this country. He had grown up believing it was the greatest thing a person could be. He had built a life here — a career, a marriage, a child. And now he was leaving, not because he wanted to but because he couldn't stay.

He didn't feel brave. He didn't feel like a pioneer or a freedom-seeker. He felt like a man who had woken up one day and realized the house he was living in had been on fire for years, and he had only just noticed the smell of smoke.

He walked to the front door and opened it.

The street was dark. The neighbors' houses were dark. The For Sale sign was still in the front lawn, a silhouette against the dim glow of the streetlight. He thought about Kevin, sitting on the porch swing, telling him he was making a mistake.

Maybe he was. Maybe this was the wrong choice. Maybe they would fail in Portugal and come back with nothing and prove everyone right.

But he had stood in a school hallway and looked at a security camera and felt something shift. And he had come home and told Marta, and she had believed him. And they had spreadsheets and phone calls and lawyers and a plan that was barely a plan, and they were doing it anyway.

He closed the door and went back to bed.

In the morning, they would leave.

---

He woke Carmen at 5 AM.

She came downstairs in her pajamas, hair tangled, eyes half-closed. The taxi was already outside, its engine running. Marta was in the kitchen, checking the bags for the third time.

"Time to go," Daniel said.

Carmen looked around the empty living room. The bare walls. The clean floors. The house that had been her home for ten years, now just a space with no furniture.

She picked up her backpack — the one she had packed herself, with her phone, her charger, a book, the small galaxy sticker she'd peeled off her closet door.

"Okay," she said.

They walked out together. Daniel locked the door behind them. The key turned in the lock with a sound he had heard a thousand times, but this time it was different.

This time, it was closing.

He put the keys in an envelope and left them in the mailbox, for the realtor. Then he got into the taxi.

Carmen was looking out the window. Marta was holding her hand. The taxi pulled away from the curb, and Daniel watched his house get smaller in the rear window, and he didn't look away until it was gone.
