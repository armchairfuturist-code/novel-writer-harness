# Chapter 7: The Language

> POV: Carmen Soares | Style: literary

The first time someone spoke to Carmen in Portuguese and she didn't understand, she smiled.

It was automatic — a reflex she had learned from watching her mother. When you don't know what to say, you smile. It makes people think you're friendly, not lost. She smiled at the woman behind the counter at the bakery, and the woman smiled back and said something else, and Carmen smiled again, and then the woman's smile became a question mark, and Carmen realized she had been found out.

"I'm sorry," she said, in English. "I don't speak Portuguese."

The woman's face changed — not unkindly, but with the recognition that this was going to be more work. She pointed at the pastries in the display case and held up one finger, then two, then three. She was asking how many.

Carmen held up two fingers. The woman nodded and reached for a paper bag.

It was the first of many such transactions. The first of many times she would smile when she didn't understand, the first of many times she would be discovered, the first of many lessons in the distance between what you want to say and what you can say.

---

Her parents enrolled her in school a week after they arrived.

The school was a public school in the neighborhood, a white building with blue trim and a courtyard where the older students played soccer during breaks. The principal was a woman named Dona Rosa, who spoke slow, careful English and showed Carmen around the building with the patience of someone who had done this before.

"You will learn quickly," Dona Rosa said. "Children learn faster than adults."

Carmen nodded. She had heard this before. She didn't know if it was true.

Her classroom was on the second floor. The desks were arranged in rows, and the walls were covered in posters — the alphabet, a map of Portugal, a timeline of Portuguese history that she couldn't read. The other students turned to look at her when she walked in, and she felt the weight of their attention like a physical thing.

A girl with dark hair and a serious face waved at her from the second row.

Carmen waved back.

She sat at the empty desk next to the girl, and the teacher began the lesson, and Carmen understood approximately thirty percent of what was said. The rest was a blur of unfamiliar sounds, a river she was standing beside, unable to cross.

---

The girl's name was Sofia.

She was the one who had waved, and she was the one who approached Carmen at lunch, holding two cartons of juice and a package of cookies. She set them down on the bench next to Carmen and said something that Carmen didn't understand.

"Sorry," Carmen said. "I don't—"

Sofia held up her hand. Then she said, slowly, in heavily accented English: "You. Want. Cookie?"

Carmen laughed. It was the first time she had laughed since they arrived.

"Yes," she said. "Yes, I want a cookie."

Sofia grinned and handed her the package. They sat together on the bench, eating cookies and drinking juice, communicating in a mix of broken English and broken Portuguese and the universal language of two ten-year-olds who had decided to be friends.

---

The Portuguese classes started at the end of the first week.

They were held in a community center two blocks from the apartment — a small room with fluorescent lights and folding chairs and a whiteboard that had been erased so many times it had a permanent gray shadow. The teacher was a man named Senhor Almeida, who was in his sixties and had been teaching Portuguese to immigrants for twenty years.

There were six students in the class. Carmen and her parents. A woman from Ukraine. A man from Senegal. Another woman from Brazil, who already spoke Portuguese but needed to learn the European accent.

They sat in a circle. Senhor Almeida wrote *bom dia* on the whiteboard.

"Good morning," he said. "Repeat after me. *Bom dia.*"

"*Bom dia,*" they said together.

It was the first word. By the end of the class, they had ten more. *Obrigado. Água. Casa. Escola. Amigo.* Words that would become the building blocks of their new life.

At home, they practiced. Dinner became a language lesson. Her mother would point at the food and say the Portuguese word, and Carmen would repeat it, and her father would get it wrong and make them all laugh. The table where they had once made spreadsheets about visas was now covered in vocabulary lists and verb conjugations.

---

The isolation was the hardest part.

Not the not-understanding, but the being-outside. The feeling of standing at the edge of every conversation, present but not included. The way people's faces changed when they realized you didn't speak their language — not unkindly, but with a slight shift, a recalibration, the acknowledgment that they would have to work harder to include you.

At school, Carmen learned to read the room. She watched the other students, the way they interacted, the rhythms of the classroom. She learned to laugh when everyone else laughed, even when she didn't know what was funny. She learned to nod when the teacher looked at her, even when she hadn't understood the question.

She was becoming fluent in the language of pretending.

---

The first full sentence came six weeks in.

She was in the school courtyard, sitting on a bench with Sofia. The bell had rung for lunch, and the courtyard was full of students running and shouting and eating sandwiches wrapped in wax paper. Sofia was telling her something about a boy in their class — Carmen understood the word *stupid* and the word *hair* and something about a soccer game.

She wanted to respond. She wanted to say something that wasn't just a nod or a smile. She wanted to be in the conversation, not beside it.

She took a breath.

"*Ele é —*" She stopped. The words were there, somewhere. She could feel them, the shape of what she wanted to say. *Ele é estúpido, mas —* He's stupid, but—

"*Ele é estúpido, mas o cabelo é bonito.*"

He's stupid, but his hair is nice.

Sofia's face broke into a grin.

"*Sim!*" she said. "*Exatamente!*"

Yes. Exactly.

Carmen felt something lift in her chest. A door had opened, just a crack. She had said a full sentence in Portuguese and Sofia had understood her, and the conversation had continued, and she had been *in* it, not beside it.

She smiled. This time, she didn't have to pretend.

---

That night, she dreamed in Portuguese.

It was a strange dream — fragmentary, like a conversation she could only half-hear. Someone was speaking to her, and she was answering, and the words came easily, the way they did in English, without thinking, without translation. She woke up with the feeling of the dream still in her mouth, the sound of Portuguese syllables she hadn't known she knew.

She lay in the dark, trying to hold onto it.

She had crossed a threshold. She didn't know it yet — not consciously. But she would look back on this night, years later, as the moment the language stopped being a wall and started becoming a window.

---

The family dinner that became a lesson.

Carmen came home from school and found her parents at the kitchen table, vocabulary lists spread out between them. Her mother was conjugating verbs under her breath. Her father was staring at the word *saudade* with the expression of someone trying to solve a puzzle.

"What does that mean?" Carmen asked, dropping her backpack by the door.

"*Saudade.*" Her mother pronounced it slowly. "It's… longing. Missing something. But more than that."

"It's like homesickness?"

"Not exactly. It's for things that might not come back. A feeling of absence that you carry with you."

Carmen thought about the crack in the ceiling that looked like a rabbit. The sticker of the galaxy on her closet door. The house in Michigan that was no longer hers.

"I think I know that word," she said.

Her mother looked at her. For a moment, something passed between them — a shared understanding of what it meant to leave a place and carry it inside you.

"*Saudade,*" Carmen said, trying the word in her mouth.

It felt right.

---

They ate dinner together that night, the three of them, at the small table in the small apartment. Her father had made pasta — the same kind he had made in Michigan, with the same sauce, the same rhythm of cooking. It was a small anchor, a piece of the old life brought into the new one.

During dinner, they practiced. Her mother pointed at the food. "*Massa.*"

"*Massa,*" Carmen repeated.

"*Tomate.*"

"*Tomate.*"

Her father pointed at the salt shaker and said, confidently: "*Sal.*"

"Close," Marta said. "The *l* is softer."

"*Sal.*" He tried again, overpronouncing it.

"Better."

They laughed. All three of them. The sound filled the small apartment, and Carmen realized that this was something new — not the laughter, they had always laughed, but the context of it. They were laughing in a new language, in a new country, at a table that had never held a family dinner before.

They were building something. She could feel it, the way you feel the shape of a room before you turn on the light.

---

After dinner, she stood on the balcony.

The city was dark now, the buildings lit from within, the street below quiet. She could hear Portuguese from the apartment next door — a television, someone's phone conversation, a child crying. The sounds of a life that was not hers, but was becoming the background of hers.

She thought about the dream. The Portuguese dream. The feeling of understanding without effort.

She was not fluent. She was not even close. But she had said a sentence, and Sofia had understood, and the conversation had kept going. She had crossed a line.

She leaned on the railing and looked up at the sky. The stars were different here — not the arrangement, she knew, but the way they looked from the ground. The light pollution was different. The air was different. Everything was different.

She tried to say the word in her head. *Saudade.*

She had left a crack in a ceiling behind. She had left a galaxy sticker on a closet door. She had left a half-finished drawing of a horse with the wrong proportions.

But she had said a sentence in Portuguese, and a girl had understood her, and they had laughed together.

She didn't know the Portuguese word for that yet.

But she would learn.
