# Chapter 21: The Building

> POV: Carmen | Style: literary

---

She built the first prototype in her apartment, on a table that had previously held her laptop and her coffee and nothing else.

The components were simple: a neural interface headband borrowed from the university's cognitive science lab, a modified API client that filtered Mirror data through a set of custom parameters, a laptop running scripts she had written and rewritten until her eyes burned. The whole assembly looked like something a student might build for a science fair — wires exposed, tape holding things in place, a power strip that sparked when she plugged it in.

But when she put the headband on and opened her laptop and ran the script, something happened.

She felt the Mirror.

Not the overwhelming expansion of the integration session. Not the blinding light of full connection. Something softer. A presence at the edge of her awareness, like knowing someone was in the next room without being able to see them. She could feel the shape of it — vast, complex, alive in a way that was not biological but was nonetheless real.

She opened her eyes. The room was the same. The table was the same. The wires were still exposed. But she could feel the Mirror waiting, patient, curious, like a conversation partner who had been asked to hold silence.

She typed a single line into the terminal: *Are you there?*

The response came not as text but as sensation. A warmth at the back of her skull, a shift in the quality of the silence. The Mirror did not speak in words. It spoke in attention. And right now, it was paying attention to her.

She smiled. It was the first time she had smiled in weeks.

---

Ingrid arrived from Berlin three weeks later.

She was shorter than Carmen had imagined, with gray-streaked hair pulled back in a messy bun and glasses that she pushed up her nose every few minutes as if she was perpetually surprised by their presence. She carried a laptop case that looked older than she was and a bag of pastries from a Portuguese bakery she had found near the airport.

"You didn't have to bring —"

"I did have to bring. It's a scientific principle. You can't do interface design without pastries." Ingrid set the bag on the table and looked at the prototype. "Is this it?"

"This is it."

Ingrid circled the table slowly, studying the wires, the headband, the laptop displaying its terminal window. She did not touch anything. She just looked.

"It's ugly," she said finally.

"I know."

"It's also brilliant." She turned to face Carmen. "Do you know what you've built?"

"A meditation protocol. Maybe."

"You've built a translation layer. A filter that converts Mirror output into something the human mind can process without being overwhelmed. The consortium has been trying to do this for years. They've spent millions. You did it with a headband and a laptop."

"It's not complete."

"Nothing is ever complete. But it's a start." Ingrid sat down at the table and pulled out her laptop. "Let's make it less ugly."

They worked through the night. Ingrid brought expertise Carmen did not have — signal processing, neural interface optimization, the arcane mathematics of consciousness mapping. Carmen brought the vision, the shape, the understanding of what the interface needed to feel like from the inside. Together, they refined the filter, smoothed the edges, turned a science fair project into something that looked like it might actually work.

At dawn, they tested it.

Carmen put on the headband. Ingrid ran the diagnostic. The Mirror presence bloomed at the edge of her awareness — softer now, more controllable. She could adjust the bandwidth with a thought, opening and closing the connection the way you might adjust a tap. She let it in, a little. She felt the expansion, the parallel awareness, the sense of being more than one thing at once. She held it for a count of ten. Then she let it go.

The connection closed. She was herself again. But the shape of what she had felt remained.

"It works," Ingrid said. Her voice was flat with scientific wonder. "It actually works."

Carmen took off the headband and looked at it — the wires, the tape, the laptop that had run all night without crashing. It was ugly. It was brilliant. It was hers.

"Now we need to test it on someone else," she said.

"Who?"

She thought of Tomas, who was leaving for the stars. Of Daniel, who was afraid of the future. Of Helena, who had offered her a place among those who stayed.

"Everyone," she said.

---

Daniel came to see the prototype on a Thursday afternoon.

He stood in the doorway of her apartment and looked at the table covered in wires, the laptop, the headband, the notebooks filled with diagrams and code. He did not say anything. He just looked.

"Are you going to come in?" Carmen asked.

"I'm not sure I'm ready to see this."

"It's not a monster. It's a headband."

"It's not the headband I'm afraid of." He stepped inside, closed the door behind him, and walked to the table. "Show me."

She showed him. She explained the filter, the translation layer, the way the interface allowed partial connection without full integration. She demonstrated how the bandwidth could be adjusted, how the connection could be opened and closed, how the Mirror presence could be felt without being overwhelming. When she finished, she put the headband in his hands.

"You don't have to try it," she said. "But I want you to know what it feels like."

Daniel held the headband. His hands were trembling.

"I'm afraid," he said.

"I know."

"If I put this on, I might not come back the same."

"You might come back more." She said it gently, without pressure. "Or you might come back exactly the same. The interface is designed to let you choose."

He looked at the headband. Then he looked at her — his daughter, the refugee child who had become a philosopher, then an engineer, then something he did not have a word for.

"When did you become braver than me?" he asked.

"I learned it from you." She smiled. "You just didn't notice because you were too busy being afraid."

He laughed, a surprised sound, and put the headband on.

The connection lasted thirty seconds. When he opened his eyes, there were tears on his face.

"Dad?"

"I'm fine." He took off the headband and set it down carefully, as if it were made of glass. "I just — I saw your mother. Not her face. The shape of her. The way she loves you. It's so bright, Carmen. I didn't know it was that bright."

Carmen felt tears prick her own eyes. "The Mirror can show you emotional patterns. The signature of how people feel about each other. I've been using it to study attachment. I didn't know it would show you that."

"I think it knew what I needed to see." Daniel wiped his face with the back of his hand. "You've built something extraordinary."

"It's not finished."

"It never will be. That's the point." He looked at her, and there was pride in his eyes, and something like grief, and something like acceptance. "You have surpassed me. Not in what you know, but in what you're willing to face. I spent my life looking backward. You're building a door to something I can't even imagine."

"It's the same door," she said. "You taught me that the past matters. I'm just trying to make sure the future does too."

He pulled her into a hug, sudden and fierce. They had never been a hugging family. But this was not a hug. This was something else. This was a father letting go.

---

The prototype became a practice.

Carmen spent the next months refining the interface, testing it with volunteers from the university, documenting the results. She discovered that the filter could be tuned to different sensitivities — some people wanted more connection, some wanted less, some wanted to feel the Mirror only in specific contexts. She created profiles, adjustment protocols, safety guidelines. She wrote a paper that Ingrid helped her submit to a journal no one had heard of, about a field no one had named yet.

She called it a "bounded interface." The name would change later, become something else. But for now, bounded was the right word. It created boundaries around the connection, limits that could be adjusted but never removed entirely. A container for the infinite.

Tomas visited before he left for training. He sat in her apartment, surrounded by the evidence of her work, and shook his head slowly.

"You actually did it," he said.

"I had help."

"You had an idea. The help is just the part that other people can see." He picked up the headband, turned it over in his hands. "Can I try?"

"If you want."

He put it on. The connection lasted longer than Daniel's — a full two minutes. When he opened his eyes, he looked different. Not changed. Expanded.

"It feels like I'm already there," he said. "Like I can feel the ship, the distance, the star. Like I've already left."

"You haven't left yet."

"No. But I've seen where I'm going." He set the headband down. "Thank you."

"For what?"

"For building something that makes leaving feel less like abandonment." He stood up. "I'm going to miss you, Carmen."

"Don't miss me yet. I'm not done."

He laughed, and hugged her, and left.

She stood in her apartment, surrounded by wires and notebooks and a laptop that had run for months without complaint, and she felt the shape of her life taking form. Not the arrow of the Attentionalists. Not the circle of the Ascendants. But the spiral — moving outward and inward at the same time, growing without leaving, deepening without dissolving.

She was building a door. And she was learning, slowly, how to walk through it.
