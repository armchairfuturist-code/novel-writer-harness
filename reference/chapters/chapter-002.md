# Chapter 2: The Spreadsheets

> POV: Marta Soares | Style: literary

She couldn't sleep so she made spreadsheets.

There was a kind of order to it that Marta's mind could hold onto when everything else was noise. The columns had to be right: *Visa Type*, *Processing Time*, *Fee*, *Success Rate*, *Notes*. The rows filled in like a grid she could see the shape of. D7 visa for freelancers and remote workers. The residency permit for those with sufficient passive income. The student visa pathway. The family reunification route that might apply through her cousin's sponsorship letter — *might*, not *would*, the difference between a door and a hallway.

The kitchen table was covered in them. Spreadsheets, printouts from government websites, a dog-eared copy of her own birth certificate that she'd had to request from the Portuguese consulate in Boston, a process that took six weeks and cost two hundred dollars and made her feel like she was applying for permission to exist in the country she'd never lived in. Her laptop glowed blue in the dark of the kitchen. The clock on the microwave said 2:14 AM. She'd been at this for three hours.

Upstairs, the house was quiet. Daniel had finally fallen asleep around midnight, after an hour of staring at the ceiling without speaking. Carmen had been in bed since nine, though Marta had checked on her twice and found her awake both times, staring at the same spot on the ceiling. They were all holding vigil over something none of them could name yet.

She opened a new tab and typed *Portugal D7 visa requirements 2026* into the search bar. The page loaded slowly — the internet had been getting slower, or maybe that was just the feeling of the whole country's infrastructure quietly degrading. She scrolled through the requirements:

- Proof of sufficient income (1.5x Portuguese minimum wage, approximately €1,200/month)
- Criminal record check
- Proof of health insurance
- Rental agreement or proof of accommodation
- Bank statements (last six months)

None of it was impossible. None of it was guaranteed either. The D7 was designed for retirees and passive-income earners, not for a systems engineer and a product director who were burning their American lives to ash in the hope of something else. They might qualify. They might not. The decision would be made by a consular officer in a windowless office in Washington, D.C., who would look at their application and decide, on a Tuesday afternoon, whether the Soares family was worth admitting to Europe.

Marta closed her eyes. The numbers on the screen glowed through her eyelids.

She thought about her father.

She didn't want to. She never wanted to. But the feeling of not being in control — of waiting for someone else to decide her fate — dragged him up from the dark like silt stirred from the bottom of a lake. The way he'd look at her across the dinner table, deciding whether she'd earned kindness or coldness that night. The way holidays were never about the holiday; they were about *his* mood, *his* generosity in pretending to be happy. The way he'd ruined every Christmas, every birthday, every family gathering — not through loud rage, but through the quiet withholding of approval, the pointed silence that made everyone in the room work to please him.

*That's the type*, she'd said to Daniel the other night, watching a politician on the news. She hadn't needed to explain. Daniel had looked at her and known exactly who she meant. He always knew. It was the thing she loved most about him and the thing she hated needing.

She opened another tab. The SEF website — Serviço de Estrangeiros e Fronteiras — the Portuguese immigration agency. She'd been through it three times already tonight. She went through it again anyway, because doing something was better than sitting with what she was feeling.

---

In the morning, Carmen found her at the table.

Marta heard her before she saw her — the particular way she came down the stairs, skipping the third step because it creaked, a habit she'd had since she was six. She appeared in the kitchen doorway in her pajamas, hair tangled, eyes still heavy with sleep.

"What are you doing?" Carmen asked.

Marta looked at the spreadsheets, the papers, the printouts. It must have looked like a disaster to a ten-year-old. It looked like a disaster to her too, but it was *her* disaster, organized and categorized.

"Checking the expiration date," she said, picking up her passport.

Carmen's eyes moved over the table. She couldn't read the abbreviations yet — DHS, USCIS, NVC — but Marta could see her trying, the way her brow furrowed the same way Daniel's did when he was working through a hard problem. She'd gotten that from him. The curiosity, the insistence on understanding.

"Is it expired?" Carmen asked.

"No. It's fine."

"Then why are you checking?"

Marta looked at her daughter. The question was innocent, but the weight of it was not. She could feel the shape of the conversation they were about to have, and she didn't know how to have it.

She set the passport down.

"Have you ever thought about what it would be like to live somewhere else?"

The words came out before she'd fully decided to say them. She watched Carmen process the question — the pause, the slight tilt of her head, the way she considered it with the seriousness of someone who had been taught, from birth, that her questions would be answered honestly.

"Like where?" Carmen asked.

"I don't know." Marta's voice was careful, deliberate. She was choosing every word. "Somewhere different. Somewhere where things are… quieter."

"Is it loud here?"

Marta looked at her daughter, and for a moment she wanted to tell her everything. The childhood she'd never spoken about. The way her father's attention had been a weather system that could turn without warning. The years she'd spent learning to read a room the way a sailor reads the sky, looking for signs of a storm. The feeling of being seven years old and knowing, with absolute certainty, that the person who was supposed to protect you was the person you needed protection from.

But you don't tell a ten-year-old that. You protect her from it. That's the whole point.

"Sometimes," she said. "In ways you can't always hear."

---

She called João at noon.

João was a former colleague from a tech conference in Berlin, 2018. They'd shared a panel on distributed systems and then a beer afterward, and he'd sent her a connection request on LinkedIn that she'd accepted and then ignored for six years. Now he was her only contact in Lisbon.

The call rang four times. She was about to hang up when he answered.

"Marta?" His voice was warm, surprised. "It's been a while."

"It has." She was standing in the kitchen, looking out the window at the backyard. The grass needed mowing. The fence needed painting. Everything was in a state of gentle disrepair, the way American things had been for a few years now, as if the whole country had stopped believing in maintenance. "I need to ask you something."

She explained it in under a minute. The school event. The thought Daniel couldn't unthink. The realization that they needed to leave. She kept her voice flat, professional, the voice she used for incident post-mortems and architecture reviews. It was the only way she could say it without falling apart.

João listened. When she finished, he was quiet for a moment.

"How soon?" he asked.

"We don't know yet. We're still figuring out the visa situation."

"I can write a letter." His voice was careful, measured. "A sponsorship letter, for the visa application. It's not a guarantee, but it helps."

"Thank you."

"It might take months. You should know that. The process has been getting slower."

"I know."

"And Marta—" He paused. "You're sure about this?"

She looked out the window. The sky was a flat, pale gray, the color of late winter in the Midwest. The house across the street had a For Sale sign in the yard that had been there for six months. The whole street felt like it was waiting for something — not in hope, but in the quiet resignation of people who had stopped believing things would get better.

"I'm sure," she said.

She hung up and stood at the window for a long time. The phone was still warm in her hand.

---

That night, Daniel found her at the kitchen table again. The same spreadsheets. The same website. The same 2 AM glow.

"You need to sleep," he said.

"I know."

He sat down across from her. He didn't say anything for a while. They had been married long enough that silence between them was not emptiness; it was a room they could both occupy.

"What's the worst case?" he asked eventually.

Marta looked at him. "We're rejected. We've spent everything on lawyers and applications. We lose the house. We have nowhere to go."

"That's not going to happen."

"You don't know that."

"I know it's not going to happen because we won't let it." He reached across the table and put his hand over hers. "We've never failed at anything we've done together."

"We've never tried to leave a country before."

"No," he said. "We haven't."

She looked at his hand on hers. The same hand that had held hers at their wedding, in the delivery room, at the kitchen table when she'd told him, three years into their marriage, about her father. She had cried telling him, and he had held her hand, and he hadn't said the wrong thing. That was when she knew she'd married the right person.

"Marta." His voice was quiet. "I need you to tell me if you don't want to do this."

"I want to."

"I need you to mean it."

She looked at him. The spreadsheet columns blurred in her peripheral vision. *Visa Type. Processing Time. Fee. Success Rate.* She had been trying to reduce the future to numbers, to make it manageable, to control the uncontrollable. But the future was not a spreadsheet. It was a leap into darkness with no guarantee of landing.

"I want to," she said again, and this time she meant it.

---

They told Carmen in pieces. Not all at once. That wasn't their way.

A week later, Daniel said the word "Portugal" to her at bedtime. Marta listened from the hallway, her back against the wall, her heart beating harder than she wanted to admit. She heard Carmen's voice, confused but not afraid. She heard Daniel's patient answers, the way he left space for her to ask questions.

She heard Carmen say: "Are we coming back?"

And she heard Daniel not answer.

Marta went back to the kitchen and opened the spreadsheet. She added a new column: *Contingency*. She filled it with numbers that made no sense, because contingency was not something you could plan for when you were already at the edge of what you could survive.

---

In the third week, the silence in the house became too loud.

Marta was at the kitchen table again — she was always at the kitchen table — but she wasn't looking at the spreadsheet. She was looking at her hands. The same hands that had held a bus ticket at seventeen, that had signed a lease on an apartment she couldn't afford, that had gripped the edge of a sink in a bathroom she'd locked herself into to cry where no one could hear. She had told herself she would never be that person again. And here she was, in a different kitchen, in a different decade, holding another invisible ticket to somewhere she didn't know.

Daniel came down for water. He stopped when he saw her face.

"Marta."

She didn't look up. "I need to tell you something. About my father."

He sat down. He didn't say anything. He knew the rule: let her speak, don't fill the space.

"There was a Christmas when I was eight," she said. "He'd been in a good mood all week. The best mood. He bought my mother a necklace. He let me stay up late. I thought — I actually thought — that maybe this time would be different." She paused. "Christmas morning, I gave him the present I'd made at school. A clay ashtray. Terrible. You could see my fingerprints in it."

"What happened?"

"He looked at it. He turned it over in his hands. And then he said: 'You expect me to use this?' Not angry. Just… disappointed. Like I'd failed a test I didn't know I was taking." She looked at Daniel. "He ruined every holiday. Not through violence. Through the quiet withdrawal of approval. You'd spend the whole week trying to earn back a warmth you weren't sure you'd ever actually had."

Daniel was quiet. Then he said: "You think that's what's happening here. The waiting. The uncertainty. You're bracing for someone to take the approval away."

She hadn't thought of it that way. But he was right. The consular officer. The visa timelines. The feeling of being evaluated by someone who could say no for reasons she would never know. It was the same shape. Different room, same shape.

"I'm not your father," he said.

"I know."

"I'm not going to take the approval away."

"Daniel." She looked at him. Her eyes were dry, but her voice was not. "I know you're not him. I've known it since the night I told you about him, and you didn't flinch, and you didn't try to fix it, and you didn't make it about yourself. You just listened. Do you understand how rare that is?"

He didn't answer. She didn't need him to.

"This is different," she said. "Leaving now, with you, with Carmen — this is not the same as running. I know the difference. I've been checking for it, the way I check a system for failure modes. And I keep coming up with the same answer."

"Which is?"

"This isn't running. This is choosing."

She reached across the table and took his hand. The same hand that had held hers for twelve years. The same hand that had been steady when hers was not.

"Okay," she said. "Let's do this."

---

The next morning, she made pancakes.

It was a small thing, but it was something she could control. The batter, the heat of the pan, the timing of the flip. She stood at the stove and let the rhythm of it steady her while the sun came up over the backyard that needed mowing.

Carmen came down the stairs, skipping the third step.

"Mami," she said. "Are we going somewhere?"

Marta didn't turn around. She focused on the pancake, watching the bubbles form on the surface, waiting for the right moment to flip.

"We're thinking about it," she said.

"Thinking about where?"

She could feel Carmen watching her. The question hung in the air. She had three weeks of spreadsheets, calls, and late-night terror behind her. She had a contact in Lisbon, a half-completed visa application, and a husband who believed in the future more than she knew how to.

She also had a daughter who was learning, from her, what it meant to face the unknown.

She turned. Carmen's face was open, curious, unknowingly brave.

"Do you remember what I asked you? About living somewhere else?" Marta said.

"Yes."

"What did you think?"

Carmen considered the question. Marta watched her think it, and felt something shift in her chest — not quite hope, but the beginning of it. A crack in the wall she'd built. A feeling she didn't have a name for yet.

"I think," Carmen said slowly, "it would be scary."

Marta looked at her daughter. At her open face. At the trust in her eyes that Marta had spent a decade trying to deserve.

"Yes," she said. "It would be."

She turned back to the pancakes. The kitchen filled with the smell of butter and heat and the ordinary comfort of a morning that felt, for the first time in weeks, like it might lead somewhere.
